🧡 Grandma-day ideas

25 fun things to do with grandkids at Grandma’s house

The best visits usually are not the most expensive or complicated ones. Kids remember small traditions, getting your attention and doing something that feels like “our thing.”

Indoor funOutdoor ideasRainy daysOlder kids too

Use this list as a menu, not a checklist. Pick one or two ideas that fit the ages of your grandkids and the amount of energy everyone has that day.

Easy indoor activities

Ages 3+1. Build a craft tray

Put out paper, stickers, crayons and glue sticks and let the kids decide what to make. A loose setup is often more fun than a complicated project.

Ages 4+2. Play a quick family game

Choose something with simple rules and a short play time so younger kids do not lose interest.

Ages 3+3. Make a blanket fort

Drape blankets over chairs, add pillows and read books or have a snack inside.

Ages 5+4. Start a puzzle together

Keep one puzzle on a card table and work on it a little each visit.

All ages5. Look through old family photos

Tell the story behind a few photos instead of flipping through hundreds. Kids often love seeing their parent as a child.

Ages 5+6. Have a “Grandma interview”

Let the grandkids ask questions about your childhood, school, favorite foods and first job. Record the answers if everyone is comfortable with that.

Kitchen activities

Ages 4+7. Bake chocolate chip cookies

Give each child a repeatable job such as pouring chocolate chips, scooping dough or setting the timer. Try Nana’s soft chocolate chip cookies.

Ages 4+8. Make pancake faces

Make pancakes and let the kids use berries, banana slices or a few chocolate chips to create faces. See our fluffy buttermilk pancake recipe.

Ages 5+9. Make a snack together

No-bake recipes are useful when you want a kitchen activity without turning on the oven. Our oat bites are one option.

All ages10. Create a family recipe card

After making something together, write the recipe title, date and who helped. Over time you will build a little family recipe collection.

Outdoor ideas

Ages 3+11. Sidewalk chalk challenge

Draw a hopscotch board, make a giant family mural or write everyone’s names in colorful letters.

Ages 3+12. Go on a neighborhood scavenger hunt

Look for a red door, a bird, a yellow flower, a mailbox, something round and something that makes noise.

Ages 4+13. Plant something together

Choose one herb, flower or vegetable and let the grandkids check on it each time they visit.

Ages 4+14. Make a bubble station

Bubbles are inexpensive, quick to set up and surprisingly entertaining across a wide range of ages.

Ages 5+15. Have a backyard picnic

The food can be ordinary. Eating it on a blanket outside makes the meal feel different.

Ages 6+16. Take a photo walk

Let each child take pictures of five things they think are beautiful, funny or interesting.

Quiet-time ideas

All ages17. Read the same special book every visit

A repeated story can become a tradition, especially with younger children.

Ages 4+18. Make a “Nana box”

Let each grandchild decorate a small box for drawings, notes or tiny treasures they want to keep at your house.

Ages 5+19. Write postcards

Send a short postcard to another family member, a cousin or even to the grandchild’s own home.

Ages 6+20. Start a shared journal

Write one sentence about what you did each visit. Add a doodle, sticker or photo when you have one.

Ideas for older grandkids and teens

Ages 10+21. Let them teach you something

Ask them to show you a game, app, hobby, song or skill they enjoy. Being the teacher can change the whole dynamic.

Ages 10+22. Cook one family recipe

Older kids can handle more of the measuring, chopping and cleanup while you tell them why the recipe matters to the family.

Ages 11+23. Watch an old movie you loved

Pick something age-appropriate from your own childhood or young-adult years and make it a conversation, not just screen time.

Ages 12+24. Go out for one-on-one time

A coffee, smoothie, bookstore visit or short walk can be more meaningful than a big outing.

Ages 10+25. Start a family project

Scan old photos, create a playlist, build a recipe book or organize family stories together over several visits.

The easiest tradition to keep is a small one.

Pick one activity you can repeat often: Friday pancakes, a puzzle table, a walk after dinner or one photo together at the end of every visit.

What if the grandkids say they are bored?

Give them two choices rather than asking an open-ended question. “Do you want to bake cookies or take a walk?” is easier to answer than “What do you want to do?” For more preparation ideas, see our guide to things to keep at Grandma’s house.
